#92. Fast, reliable A2UI, with swappable inference formats

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

We've heard your concerns about the speed of A2UI and generative UI experiences: people wait while the model generates each screen. This release gives you levers to make that faster, so you can choose the trade-off that fits each screen.

With this release, we’re happy to introduce swappable inference formats and share examples on leveraging decision models with A2UI, which help building faster and more reliable generative UI experiences. Using our new A2UI Express as an inference format you can reduce latency by up to 3x, and up-to 80x using decision models like Jev to make layout decisions. Express requires up to 3x fewer tokens to generate the same kinds of UI compared to Direct JSON. We'll dive into the details in this blog post.

A2UI is an agent-optimized protocol for generative UI, where a client advertises a catalog of trusted components as a contract, an agent sends A2UI messages describing a bespoke user interface composed from that catalog, and the client's renderer draws the UI (see Introducing A2UI).

Initially agents generated A2UI JSON transport messages directly, every curly brace and nested element. It turns out that generating JSON trees constrained by a complex JSON schema is slow and error-prone for LLMs. This coupled A2UI to LLMs, which output text, and made it difficult to integrate faster System One decision models.

Now agents can generate A2UI Express, an intermediate format optimized for inference speed and accuracy, and rely on deterministic code in A2UI's Agent SDKs to translate it into the JSON-based transport protocol. Agent builders are also free to swap in any other DSL and its associated compiler for any special use-cases, and our A2UI Agent SDK(s) make it compatible with transports and renders.

The format the model generates is what we call inference_format. You set it with one argument when you create the generator. The SDK generates the prompt instructions for that format and compiles the model's reply into standard A2UI messages. This post covers how the swap works, why we developed A2UI Express as the new default inference format, and how a decision model can build screens from layouts you define in advance.

An inference format has two parts, and the SDK generates both from your catalog's JSON schema. A prompt section teaches the model the format's syntax, and a compiler compiles the model's reply into validated A2UI messages. Because both come from the schema, a format can't describe a component or property that your catalog lacks.

A2UI Express is available in the Python agent SDK (a2ui-agent-sdk 0.9.0 on PyPI), in the TypeScript agent SDK (@a2ui/agent on npm), and in the Dart SDK (a2ui_agent 0.0.1-wip004 on pub.dev). In TypeScript, you pass new ExpressFormatFactory() as the generator's third argument. The Python and TypeScript SDKs can both stream Direct JSON chunk by chunk, and they compile Express from the complete reply.

Express writes one statement per line, in a style close to Python. Each line assigns a component to a variable, and the variable name becomes the component ID. Arguments are positional: the compiler maps them to property names in the order your catalog schema declares them, and _ skips one. $/title binds a value to the data model, and Event("accept") declares a server action. Some screens have a known shape, such as a settings page, a checkout or a confirmation. For those, the model doesn't need to design a layout. It needs to choose among layouts you have already defined, and a different kind of model is built for that. For this, you would not need the full expressivity provided by A2UI Express.

Jev, from TypeSafe, is what is called a System One model, built to make fast, structured decisions that code can use directly. Jev doesn't generate text or JSON. You send it a state and a set of typed questions, and it answers each one with calibrated probabilities. A question can ask whether a statement is true (Noul), which option from a list fits (Choice), or how something scores on a rubric (Score). Jev answers many questions in one request, in roughly 100 to 300 ms.

#13. <a href="https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/payers/look-how-employers-are-approaching-ai-enabled-benefits-solutions" hreflang="en">A look at how employers are approaching AI-enabled benefits solutions</a>

📅 Oct 28, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
A look at how employers are approaching AI-enabled benefits solutions" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

Most employers are weighing the potential of AI-enabled benefits programs and how best to offer them to workers, according to a new survey.

Experts at Aon and Define Ventures polled 67 human resources and benefits leaders across multiple industries and representing a variety of employer sizes, and found that 17% currently have live AI deployments where they have the capacity to change workflows. The results were provided first to Fierce Healthcare.

In addition, 30% said they are mulling AI deployments and named it a top priority, but have yet to sign on with a vendor. In this group, most cited privacy concerns as the key reason for their hesitancy. Twenty-four percent said they're leaning more on their existing partners to deliver AI tools, rather than seeking new partners for the technology.

And 20% said they have no plans for AI-enabled benefits in the near term but see the potential in improving the employee experience, the survey found. These leaders said the potential cost and return on investment for these platforms slowed their pace of adoption.

Kevin Fyock, innovation and strategy leader for health Solutions – North America at Aon, said in a statement that analysts expect employers to develop more defined evaluation standards for AI-powered benefits, and they'll likely be looking for stronger evidence before signing on with a vendor partner.

"As employers consider AI-enabled benefits solutions, the conversation is increasingly centered on trust and accountability," said Fyock. "They want to understand not only what a solution can do but also how it protects employee information, supports accurate and reliable outputs and fits within existing governance and procurement processes."

"Employers are also becoming more discerning about ‘AI washing’ and will expect vendors to clearly explain which capabilities are AI-enabled and substantiate the claims they make," he said.

In the group that was currently using AI-enabled benefits, 51% were piloting new solutions while 49% were taking a more partner-led approach and leaning on existing vendors, the survey found.

Meanwhile, 61% of the employers who were not currently deploying AI in their benefits strategies said they were exploring the possibility. Thirty-nine percent had no plans.

The tech is most often being utilized at present to address key friction points like employee communication, support around open enrollment and ways to streamline benefits employees' workflows. These areas have some of the strongest potential to pay off in the near-term, per the survey, and also do not require that the benefits teams tailor data to specific individual employees, rather allowing them to work from a single source of truth.

To establish a stronger partnership, the study found that AI companies should start by clearly defining what the employer views as value and building the deployment around their priorities. The survey identified multiple focus areas for employers, ranging from the expectation of demonstrated ROI to key technical and governance guardrails.

Chirag Shah, partner at Define Ventures, said in a statement that given AI's "dramatic effect on the healthcare landscape" the tech holds plenty of potential for employers to unlock.

"Health systems, payers and providers—three of the major buyers in our industry—has reaped significant benefits, which Define’s own surveys and reports have demonstrated over the last several years," Shah said. "More recently, we have seen similar dynamics appearing in the employer ecosystem: we believe the potential for AI to impact both benefits leaders and the employee healthcare experience at large is tremendous."

#16. <a href="https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/payers/work-requirements-state-directed-payments-front-and-center-namd-2026" hreflang="en">Work requirements, state-directed payments front-and-center at NAMD 2026</a>

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →
Work requirements, state-directed payments front-and-center at NAMD 2026" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

The impact of H.R. 1 was front and center at the Medicaid directors' conference, NAMD, last week in Maryland.

The conference, hosted by the National Association of Medicaid Directors, gathered officials and attendees from both sides of the aisle. The agenda focused largely on policy and technology.

At a press briefing during the event, several state Medicaid officials spoke about the upcoming Medicaid work requirement changes going into effect on Jan. 1, 2027. Already starting this month, around 10,000 New Jersey legal immigrants lost coverage in the state. New Jersey is reaching out to 25,000 more members to request additional information on their eligibility status. For those not eligible, said New Jersey Medicaid Director Greg Woods, “where can we have continuity of care and access to care, and with a particular focus on vulnerable populations?”

“This is not a normal build for us,” Woods said of the IT development underway by the state to accommodate H.R. 1 changes. “The timeline is vastly accelerated. […] I don’t think on January 1, we’ll be in our final landing place.”

New Jersey is also working with counties to ensure they have the capacity and budget to handle the changes. Woods expects their workload to increase tremendously.

The situation is similar in Illinois, where the Medicaid department is doing IT work under “extraordinarily tight timelines,” echoed Laura Phelan, the state’s Medicaid administrator. As in New Jersey, over 9,000 immigrants lost coverage at the start of this month. The state projects that up to 300,000 residents could ultimately lose coverage.

“Coverage loss due to H.R. 1 changes also will put substantial financial pressure on individuals, providers and the broader healthcare system,” Phelan said. The state is facing up to a $51 billion loss in the next decade, largely due to provider tax changes and new eligibility rules, she added.

Member outreach is a priority for states. Louisiana can determine whether members are exempt from the new requirements for the vast majority of members based on existing data. However, for about 10% to 20% of members, the state does not have enough data to make that determination. Part of the state’s plan is to leverage AI to respond to basic member questions, such as application status, “so that we can deploy call center staff more effectively,” said Seth Gold, Louisiana’s executive Medicaid director.

Compliance with H.R. 1’s fiscal provisions—which prohibit new provider taxes, reduce the allowable cap on existing ones and impose limits on state-directed payments—will have a significant impact on New Jersey. “When it is fully ramped up,” Woods said, “there will be a several-billion-dollar annual impact on our healthcare system.”

Meanwhile, Tennessee faces impact from both state-directed payments and budget neutrality. In 2025, it implemented a significant state-directed payment to hospitals, bringing them up to average commercial rates. In 2028, the state will have to ration that down to 110% of Medicare.

“It’s certainly less than what they were anticipating,” acknowledged Stephen Smith, director of TennCare, the state’s Medicaid program. However, he added, even with the reductions, “Tennessee hospitals will be receiving more from Medicaid than they ever have.”

For years, Tennessee has had a unique shared-savings model, TennCare III. The federal government caps the total amount of federal Medicaid funds it receives, and if it spends under the cap, it can share in some of those savings. In the first four years of the 10-year waiver, it generated $1.3 billion in shared savings. The state was able to provide diapers for low-income children through Medicaid. It has also committed over $300 million for rural health initiatives and transferred $100 million in no-interest disaster relief loans to counties hit by Hurricane Helene.

“The whole concept is based on our budget neutrality cap,” Smith said. “This has been incredibly successful [for the state].”

The new H.R. 1 policy requires states’ demonstration project waivers to be “budget neutral,” meaning a state’s proposal would not result in costs to the federal government greater than what Medicaid costs would be without it. Some legal experts have suggested that while federal Medicaid spending may become more predictable, this new rule may also limit the use of waivers to expand Medicaid eligibility and benefits.

At the same time, Smith is excited about a new four-year pilot, approved earlier this year, to incentivize Medicaid members to accept a higher-paying job or promotion without fear of losing Medicaid benefits. The Pathway to Independence pilot, set to start in 2027, will offer time-limited premium assistance on the private market to the parent-caretaker population.

“We don’t know if this is going to work, but we do know that if it does work … then it’ll be tremendous, because it will help the people that we serve, but it will also save taxpayers a tremendous amount of dollars,” Smith said.

A session set up as a debate over federal flexibility, fiscal sustainability and access to care featured experts with opposing ideological views. Brian Blase, president of the conservative think tank, Paragon Health Institute, lamented cost-shifting from states to Washington, claiming that 50% to 90% of state spending is reimbursed by the federal government.

Blase previously served as a health policy advisor in the first Trump administration. He said states give in to lobbying to expand Medicaid “because they can pass most of the bill to Washington.” Paragon previously campaigned hard against provider taxes and state-directed payments, hospital-friendly Medicaid mechanisms it described as “gimmicks,” efforts that, per reports, found an ear inside the administration and Congress.

“There is a lot of corporate welfare in Medicaid that is not going to the most vulnerable,” Blase said, noting that in some states, Medicaid rates approach commercial rates for what he called politically powerful hospitals. He also said COVID-era policies and Medicaid expansion have “exacerbated” waste, fraud and abuse in the system. These issues have been a core focus for the Trump administration and a salient message for many voters in the run-up to the midterms.

Meanwhile, Daniel Tsai, director of health for the City & County of San Francisco, argued Medicaid is the lowest-cost insurance in the country. Tsai was previously director of Medicaid & CHIP at CMS under President Biden.

He stated that on a per-member, per-month basis, Medicaid costs have not increased in 20 years. “Many providers do not even take Medicaid,” Tsai said. Unless every health system is required equally to take Medicaid, cuts to the program effectively crater the safety net for vast swaths of the population, he added.

Tsai called for broad policy changes at the national, state and provider levels. He acknowledged that most Medicaid variation is not purposeful, adding that “it makes it freaking difficult for patients … to actually navigate the system.” And while the program has been great for two decades, "I think we need to rethink the role of managed care.” For example, the system spends $45 billion annually on administrative overhead related to managed care.

When it comes to H.R. 1's impact on communities, Joan Alker, executive director and research professor at Georgetown University Center for Children & Families, is concerned about the uninsured. These cuts will not just impact adults, but also kids, people with disabilities and older adults. “I’m extremely worried about systems literally collapsing,” she said on the panel. She is also worried about states’ capacity to handle the new rules amid staffing shortages and a “very chaotic federal partner.”

Alker believes issues like rising healthcare costs and an aging population must be addressed with federal action. “We have a healthcare costs problem, not a Medicaid costs problem,” she said. Alker believes staffing up Medicaid offices right now would be cost-saving. “But let’s be clear: no matter how hard you work,” she addressed the audience, “you cannot overcome the systemic challenges that Medicaid is being asked to address.”

Alker's comments garnered numerous rounds of applause from the full ballroom of conference attendees. Paragon's Blase received none.

In a fireside chat, Dan Brillman, deputy administrator & director of the Centers for Medicaid & CHIP Services (CMCS), spoke about his priorities for the program, focusing a lot on the idea of value.

"I want outcomes," he said. "Getting value for every dollar we spend is super important."

Brillman has a business background, having been co-founder and CEO of the health tech company Unite Us. That he was a top contender for the CMCS role was first reported by Fierce Healthcare.

Among Brillman's top priorities is streamlining program design. "When we learn something that works well in Medicaid, I get frustrated that it doesn't freaking scale," he said. Promoting technology is one potential solution. Another is simplifying NCQA measures.

A May 2026 CMS analysis of Medicaid managed care programs across 42 states found about 450 reporting requirements related to about 260 unique quality measures for federal and state-specific quality reporting programs. These were found to be largely focused on process and utilization.

Brillman said collecting these data is "performative." Instead, he said states should move toward fewer, more relevant measures. Having fewer measures would also reduce the reporting burden.

"Data should not just be a reporting function," Brillman said.

On 1115 waivers, Brillman said that they are intended to be five-year pilots that try out interventions. If they work, then it's up to states to lobby Congress to make them a concrete benefit. Whatever benefit is identified should be clinically linked and cost-saving.

"We have to prove these things work and get to the right population," Brillman said, such as a patient with congestive heart failure, diabetes and food insecurity. Brillman praised accountable care for its ability to outperform fee-for-service on outcomes. But some states are questioning whether they should have managed care at all, he said, which is concerning. He believes the program needs improvement, claiming it's inefficient to compete on rates. He called on payers and consultants to step up and take advantage of the current momentum for change.

In an attempt to coalesce states around quality measures, CMCS recently launched an initiative with 37 state partners. The goal is to focus on how quality is measured and center outcomes in the work. CMS and its partners will prioritize measures that demonstrate meaningful improvements in prevention, chronic disease management and behavioral health. CMS also plans to hold additional workshops this year on quality measurement. The effort, Investing in Health Outcomes, asks states to pledge to put health outcomes above processes, streamline quality measures without reducing accountability, push toward digital quality measurement and align financial results with outcomes.

Brillman closed out the fireside chat with a call to action related to waste, fraud and abuse for attendees, saying he would rather have collaborative conversations early to proactively address a burgeoning issue, before it blows up into a major problem. "I'd rather be a partner than a regulator," Brillman said.

#24. <a href="https://www.fiercehealthcare.com/payers/alignment-healthcare-sues-cms-over-its-2027-medicare-advantage-star-ratings" hreflang="en">Aetna sues to challenge 2027 MA star ratings</a>

📅 Oct 27, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →
Aetna sues to challenge 2027 MA star ratings" style="max-width:100%; height:auto; border-radius:8px;" loading="lazy">

CVS Heath's Aetna is joining the ranks of insurers suing over its results in the 2027 Medicare Advantage star ratings.

The insured filed suit Monday in Georgia's Southern District Court, and argues that 28 measures used by the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services to calculate its scores were "unlawfully adopted" and should have been excluded.

The court is the same venue that ruled in favor of Clover Health over the summer, and set aside 20 measures that were used to determine its 2026 scores. That decision, which the feds have appealed, kicked off a broad recalculation of 2026 scores that looked different for insurers outside of Clover, triggering a wave of litigation.

In its lawsuit, Aetna cites the Clover decision in finding that CMS extended beyond its statutory authority in deploying those 20 measures.

The company said that calculating its scores using the 28 measures in question will likely cost it $2 billion in 2028 quality bonus payments and rebate retention. Those are funds that the insurer could "apply toward expanding member benefits, reducing premiums and investing in quality improvement."

Aetna adds that in addition to the monetary loss, having lower scores negatively impacts the company's reputation and its ability to attract potential members.

"The harm to CVS Health is not exclusively financial; lower ratings carry meaningful reputational harm, which can in turn impact membership," per the lawsuit. "By incorporating unlawful measures into the 2027 Star Ratings calculation, CMS cast CVS Health’s ratings in a poorer light in the eyes of Medicare Advantage consumers relative to peer MAOs, undermining CVS Health’s ability to compete for Medicare beneficiaries through enhanced benefits and the like."

Aetna did not respond to a request for comment at the time of publication.

Based on an analysis from Stephens, 69% of Aetna's MA members are in plans with four or more stars for 2027, down from 81%. That threshold is the key to securing quality bonus payments.

Aetna's complaint follows others focused on the 2027 scores from Centene and Alignment Healthcare (see those stories below).

Centene has become the latest insurer to sue the federal government over its 2027 Medicare Advantage star ratings.

In a lawsuit filed Thursday in Georgia's Southern District Court, the company argues that the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services violated the Administrative Procedure Act in determining its latest scores. Centene says that CMS ran afoul of "core statutory safeguards" in deploying 17 measures.

The use of those measures ultimately caused "significant injury" to the company, according to the complaint. For 2027, 9% of Centene's members are enrolled in plans with at least four stars, which declined from 18% in the prior year.

"In implementing each of the individual Star measures that Centene challenges, CMS violated applicable law and thereby caused Centene to lose out on hundreds of millions of dollars in revenue that Centene could use to strengthen benefits, reduce premiums, and improve health outcomes," the company said in the lawsuit.

CMS argues that the court has already sided with a similar argument in its decision in favor of Clover Health earlier this year. In that case, which led to a broad recalculation in scores, the courts set aside 20 measures that were used in determining Clover's star ratings for 2026.

If the 17 measures in question had been excluded in calculating Centene's scores, the company estimates it would receive $448 million more in quality bonus payments for the 2028 plan year, funds it would deploy to expand benefits, lower premiums and enhance programs like care coordination.

In addition to the financial impact, a drop in star ratings also carries a reputational hit, Centene said.

"Beyond the financial impact to the company and its members, depressed ratings also inflict significant reputational damage and diminished membership," the company said in the complaint

Centene had not returned a request for comment at the time of publication.

Alignment Healthcare sued federal regulators, alleging that its 2027 Medicare Advantage star ratings were determined "in flagrant violation" of statute.

In the lawsuit filed Friday in District of Columbia federal court, the insurer says that the Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services "has completely departed from Congress’s vision of leveraging predictable, valid measures developed with input from providers, plans, and the public" in establishing the methodology for the star ratings.

The company argues that rather than establish "consistent, stable specifications" for metrics through the traditional rule-making process, the agency instead detailed key measures through a series of "technical notes." This violates the Administrative Procedure Act, per the lawsuit.

The program's star ratings are the critical determinant in quality bonus payments to insurers as well as a tool that aims to make it easier for enrollees and brokers to identify benefits with the highest quality.

"Consistent application of the statutory requirements governing Star Ratings is therefore essential not only to plan payments and competition but also to ensuring that beneficiaries can rely on the Star Ratings as meaningful and comparable measures of plan quality," Alignment said in the lawsuit.

The company adds that multiple recent court decisions have set aside CMS' star ratings methodology, notably a ruling over the summer in favor of Clover Health that tossed 20 metrics used in the 2026 calculations. A Georgia federal judge determined that 10 measures utilized exceeded CMS' authority, while 10 other measures were promulgated inappropriately.

CMS recalculated the scores across the board following the ruling, but only set aside the 10 measures that the courts determined were outside the bounds of its regulatory authority. The agency maintained that approach in the 2027 calculations, Alignment argues.

"Congress created the Star Ratings program to allow Medicare Advantage beneficiaries to make informed decisions about plan enrollment based on clinically validated measures of plan quality that had been subjected to notice and comment," the company said. "CMS’s contrary approach fails to carry out the vision that Congress set forth by statute."

The Trump administration has appealed the Clover decision, so that legal case remains ongoing.

In addition, Alignment argues it suffered reputational and financial damage as a result of its 2027 scores, which plummeted compared to the prior year. Just 25% of its members are in plans with four or more stars for 2027, down from 100% in 2026.

Shares in the company tumbled following the release of the ratings, though stock prices have rebounded since.

Alignment sued the feds following the Clover decision to secure a recalculation for its scores that aligns with the court's decision, and in a statement to Fierce Healthcare, said that the lawsuit aligns with that perspective.

"Our lawsuit seeks to ensure that the Star Ratings program operates as Congress intended and that Alignment is properly compensated for the care we provide," Alignment said in the statement. "We respect the judicial process and will present our case in court. Because this matter involves pending litigation, we will not comment on the specific legal arguments beyond our filings."

#6. This Is How Fast You Should Walk to Help Lower Your Risk of Death

📅 Oct 26, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

New research shows that how fast you walk, whether measured in a clinical test or through daily activity, can help predict your risk of dying from any cause.

The study compared two ways of measuring walking: a short walking speed test conducted in a clinic and the step rate people take in their everyday lives, measured with a wrist-worn device.

Both measures were linked to mortality risk and showed similar ability to predict it.

The findings, published on October 23 in JAMA Network Open, could help doctors and researchers choose the best method for assessing health risks in different settings.

The study was part of the Whitehall II accelerometer substudy in the United Kingdom.

It included 4,067 participants, mostly males, with an average age of about 69 years. About one-third of the subjects were female.

The researchers collected data from January 2012 to December 2013 and followed up through August 2024.

To measure clinical walking speed, participants completed a standard walking test. They walked a marked 2.44-meter course at their usual pace. The test was repeated three times, and the average speed in meters per second was used for analysis. Participants who normally used a cane were allowed to use it during the test.

For free-living walking measurements, participants wore a wrist-worn GENEActiv accelerometer for 9 consecutive days. This device recorded movement at a high frequency and used special software to detect walking episodes and count steps.

From this data, two key measures of walking cadence were calculated: the median walking cadence (the middle value of steps per minute during walking periods) and the 95th-percentile walking cadence (a higher step rate indicating brisk walking). The study also calculated the average number of daily steps.

The researchers additionally gathered information on factors known to affect mortality, such as:

National death records were used to identify deaths during the follow-up period.

Statistical methods were then used to estimate the risk of death associated with walking speed and cadence while accounting for other risk factors.

The models were adjusted step-by-step: starting with no adjustments, then adding sociodemographic factors, behavioral factors, and finally health-related factors.

The ability of walking measures to predict mortality was assessed using a statistical measure.

The study also examined differences between males and females.

The researchers additionally conducted analyses to test how well the results held up when they excluded early deaths, looked at subgroups by age and health status, and used different definitions of walking cadence.

During a median follow-up of nearly 12 years, 739 participants died.

The average clinical walking speed of participants was 1.2 meters per second. The median walking cadence during daily life was about 75 steps per minute, and the 95th percentile walking cadence was about 112 steps per minute.

The study found only a weak correlation between clinical walking speed and free-living walking cadence, meaning the two measures do not strongly reflect the same walking behavior. However, both measures were independently associated with a lower risk of death.

After adjusting for age, sex, lifestyle, and health factors, for every standard increase in clinical walking speed, the risk of death was 23% lower.

Similarly, higher median and 95th-percentile walking cadences were associated with 17% and 18% lower risks of death, respectively.

When examining how well these walking measures predicted mortality, each on its own showed modest predictive ability.

Adding clinical walking speed to a model with other known risk factors slightly improved mortality prediction, while adding free-living walking cadence also improved prediction, albeit to a similar degree to that of clinical walking speed.

Combining clinical walking speed with free-living walking parameters provided a small boost in predictive ability, but the effect was not significant after accounting for detailed health factors.

The study also found that walking speed and cadence had similar predictive power for both men and women, although the overall prediction models performed slightly better for women.

Several sensitivity analyses supported the main findings. Results were consistent when excluding early deaths, focusing on healthier or older participants, and using different ways to define walking cadence.

Overall, this study suggests that clinical walking speed tests and free-living walking cadence measurements from wrist-worn devices are both useful in predicting all-cause mortality risk. They provide similar information but reflect different aspects of walking behavior.

The choice between methods may depend on the purpose of the assessment, available resources, and the context in which the measurement is taken.

Neha Sabharwal, PT, DPT, a physical therapist and director of rehabilitation specializing in geriatric mobility and functional decline at Vintage Faire Nursing and Rehabilitation Center in Modesto, CA, said that walking speed helps predict longevity because it’s an indicator of someone’s overall health.

“Walking is something most of us do without thinking about it, but it requires several body systems to work together,” she told Healthline. “Your heart and lungs have to keep up, your muscles need to produce enough force, and your brain has to coordinate each step while keeping you balanced.”

Sabharwal, who wasn’t involved in the study, said that in her work, she pays attention to when someone starts walking more slowly than before.

“It is easy to dismiss that change as part of getting older, but there may be something else going on,” she said. This could include muscle weakness, pain, reduced endurance, balance problems, or underlying medical conditions.

According to Sabharwal, the current study is valuable because it suggests that useful information about people’s movement can be obtained outside a clinical setting.

However, she cautioned against misinterpreting the findings to mean that simply walking faster will make you live longer.

“People who naturally walk faster may already have better underlying health,” said Sabharwal.

“I would be less concerned about whether someone walks faster than their neighbor and more interested in whether they are walking slower than they did six months ago,” she said.

Theodore Strange, MD, chairman of medicine at Northwell Staten Island University Hospital and senior vice president of the Metro Market Region at Northwell Health, shared some insights for those who may notice their walking speed has declined. Strange wasn’t involved in the study.

“I mean, if it’s slowing down because a week or two ago, you were walking up a flight of steps with no problem, and now, you’re having some shortness of breath, I think it should be evaluated,” he told Healthline.

Strange added that if you’re experiencing aches or pains that weren’t there before, you should consult a healthcare professional, as they can make exercise more difficult.

It’s also essential to manage any chronic diseases that you have so that you can exercise as well as possible within the constraints of your condition, he said.

“And I think slow and steady wins the race here,” said Strange. He suggests monitoring your walking with a device like a pedometer and progressively increasing your steps.

“Everybody talks about 10,000 steps a day. I think the most recent studies show probably 7,000 to 8,000 is adequate,” he said. You can start where you are and work your way up gradually.

Strange further noted that physical therapy can sometimes be helpful if you have issues with gait mechanics, balance, or strength. Additionally, you might benefit from exercise that is tailored to your condition, for example, if you’ve had a stroke.

Finally, Strange advised stretching before and after exercise to keep your muscles moving.

“I go back to adequate nutrition and hydration being important,” he said. You want to make sure you are adequately supporting your body’s ability to exercise, said Strange.

“Hepatitis B immunizations have been proven to protect individuals against a serious and significant disease,” said Graham Tse, MD, pediatrician and chief medical officer of MemorialCare Miller Children’s & Women’s Hospital in Long Beach, CA.

“The hepatitis B vaccines have several decades of history and have been proven safe and effective,” Tse said. “There has been no new data or evidence presented or uncovered to support delaying the newborn dose of hepatitis B.”

A U.S. modeling study published in Annals of Internal Medicine on October 20 projected that delaying the first vaccine dose until 2 months could increase chronic hepatitis B infections among infants by 19% under one scenario.

With shared decision making and lower vaccine uptake, projected increases ranged from 83% to 225%, compared with universal birth-dose vaccination.

The researchers also estimated that up to 1.8 million young adults could remain susceptible to hepatitis B by age 20 under shared decision making, compared with 232,000 under universal birth dosing.

Another study published in JAMA Pediatrics in late April projected hundreds of additional chronic infections annually if the hepatitis B birth dose were delayed or omitted.

However, these figures are model-based projections, rather than verified outcomes. Actual results will depend on vaccination rates and other factors.

In December 2025, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) adopted a recommendation shifting away from universal hepatitis B vaccination at birth for infants whose mothers test negative for hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg).

Under the new approach, parents and healthcare professionals can decide together whether to give the first dose at birth or defer it until the baby is 2 months old.

The change followed an 8 to 3 vote by the Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices (ACIP), marking a departure from longstanding U.S. guidance.

However, infants born to mothers who test positive for hepatitis B or whose infection status is unknown should still receive the hepatitis B vaccine and hepatitis B immune globulin (HBIG) within 12 hours of birth, according to the updated guidelines.

Hepatitis B is a viral infection that affects the liver.

Although some infections resolve, newborns and infants face a higher risk of developing lifelong hepatitis B infection compared with adults.

Up to 90% of infants infected during their first year develop chronic hepatitis B.

“We have actual world evidence that vaccinating newborns for hepatitis B prevents hepatitis B infections later in life and the serious complications of that disease,” Tse said.

Over time, chronic infection can lead to cirrhosis, liver failure, or liver cancer.

“The danger of delaying the newborn hepatitis B shot is that individuals do not get vaccinated and thereby do not have protection against hepatitis B,” Tse said.

Early vaccination helps protect young infants before potential exposure occurs, including situations in which maternal screening misses an infection.

“Newborns can be infected with hepatitis B from a mother who is not aware they have a hepatitis B infection or from exposure later in life,” Tse said.

“The risk is developing a hepatitis B infection, which can potentially lead to liver cancer, fulminant hepatitis, or severe end-stage liver disease.”

In contrast to the CDC, the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP) still recommends a universal hepatitis B vaccine dose within 24 hours of birth, followed by additional doses during infancy.

The hepatitis B vaccine has decades of safety and effectiveness data.

Tse said the AAP’s vaccine schedule and recommendations are based on decades of study, use, and expert input, and provide the best protection against vaccine-preventable diseases.

Parents who have questions about potential side effects, timing, or their baby’s individual risk should discuss them with a qualified healthcare professional.

The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG) published updated recommendations for menopause management in Obstetrics & Gynecology on October 15.

These expanded recommendations are the most comprehensive guidance to date. They include new clinical guidelines for menopause hormone therapy (MHT), nonhormonal medications, and nonpharmacological treatment of vasomotor symptoms (VMS), which include hot flashes and night sweats, and sleep and mood changes.

According to the ACOG guidance, 50%–92% of females experiencing menopause report VMS.

Around 16%–66% of females in the United States report menopause-related sleep issues, and more than one-third of perimenopausal and postmenopausal females report experiencing depressive symptoms.

“In recent years, misconceptions about the risks of hormone therapy have been widespread, which has made some clinicians reluctant to prescribe it and some patients hesitant to use it,” Bruce Pier, MD, clinical professor and associate fellowship director at the University of North Carolina and collaborator on the ACOG clinical practice guidelines, told Healthline.

“This guidance offers a comprehensive review of the evidence to support individualized counseling and shared decision making. Our hope is that it clears up those misconceptions and expands access to treatment.”

The ACOG is also developing companion guidance for genitourinary syndrome of menopause, expected in 2027.

The ACOG strongly recommends the use of MHT with estrogen and progestogens (or estrogen alone for people without a uterus) for both VMS and vasomotor-related sleep disturbance, in individuals who can use hormones.

It also conditionally recommends conjugated estrogen plus bazedoxifene (Duavee) as an alternative for these issues.

According to a meta-analysis from 2021 that was cited by the guidelines, estrogen therapy, with or without progestogens, significantly reduced VMS frequency and severity compared to a placebo or no treatment.

#45. Increasing Ozempic Dose Linked to Lower Heart Risk Than Switching to Mounjaro

📅 Oct 05, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

Increasing the dose of Ozempic rather than switching to Mounjaro may be associated with a lower risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), according to a 2026 insurance claims analysis presented at the European Association for the Study of Diabetes (EASD) Annual Meeting 2026 in Milan, Italy.

GLP-1 medications like semaglutide (Ozempic) and tirzepatide (Mounjaro) have reshaped care for people with type 2 diabetes, but recent real-world evidence suggests these drugs may not offer identical heart protection.

It is important to keep in mind that this was a retrospective, observational study that showed an association, not causation.

More research is needed to fully compare the cardiovascular benefits of semaglutide versus tirzepatide.

The COMPETE SWITCH Cardiovascular (CV) analysis used Komodo Health’s Healthcare Map, a large US healthcare claims database with linked laboratory results, to examine real-world data from 636,525 adults with type 2 diabetes.

The analysis focused on 185,705 people who increased their semaglutide dose and 23,104 who switched to tirzepatide.

All participants included in the analysis were already receiving 1 mg of semaglutide (Ozempic) weekly.

The researchers found that people who increased their Ozempic dose to 2 mg had a 6% lower risk of MACE — including all-cause death, myocardial infarction (heart attack), and stroke — compared with those who switched to Mounjaro at doses up to 15 mg.

“This is an interesting study, but the findings may partly reflect medication adherence. When patients switch medications, it can take time to adjust the dose and find the right treatment level, which may temporarily affect how well their condition is controlled,” said Mir Ali, MD, medical director of MemorialCare Surgical Weight Loss Center at Orange Coast Medical Center in Fountain Valley, CA, who wasn’t involved in the study.

He also noted that the study did not determine what might explain the difference in cardiovascular risk.

“It may be related to a combination of weight loss and direct effects on hormones involved in metabolism and appetite regulation,” Ali said.

Semaglutide and tirzepatide are both GLP-1 medications. Ozempic (semaglutide) and Mounjaro (tirzepatide) are approved to help manage type 2 diabetes, while each medication also has a different brand approved for weight loss.

The semaglutide approved for weight loss in the U.S. is sold as Wegovy, and the tirzepatide approved for weight loss is sold as Zepbound.

“Semaglutide and tirzepatide work in a similar way. While semaglutide is a direct GLP-1 receptor agonist, tirzepatide is a dual agonist of both the GIP and GLP-1 receptors,” Amit Blumfield, MD, with Manhattan Cardiology in NYC, who wasn’t involved in the study, told Healthline.

“Because they work through somewhat different pathways, they can have different effects in individual patients,” added Ali.

While both semaglutide (Ozemipc) and tirzepatide (Mounjaro) work in similar ways, you may find that you tolerate one better than the other.

Ali noted that you should discuss any potential benefits and drawbacks with your healthcare professional.

“I would only recommend switching medications if there is an issue with tolerance of one or the other. If one medication is well-tolerated at increasingly higher doses and is effective in the desired weight-loss and blood sugar management, I wouldn’t necessarily switch,” added Blumfield.

These side effects are generally mild and may lessen over time. However, if side effects become difficult to tolerate, you should speak with your healthcare professional.

“GLP-1 medications can be highly effective for managing diabetes and promoting weight loss, but there is no one-size-fits-all treatment. The best medication depends on an individual’s health needs, goals, response to treatment, and potential side effects,” said Ali.

#47. Can the BPC-157 ‘Wolverine’ Peptide Really Help With Pain and Recovery?

📅 Oct 04, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

Wellness seekers are increasingly turning to BPC-157, the wildly popular “Wolverine” peptide for injury recovery and pain relief, despite scant human evidence.

The Food and Drug Administration (FDA) advisory panel voted in July to ease restrictions on it and several other peptides, recommending the injectable substances be made eligible for pharmacy compounding.

But before restrictions were eased, preliminary research suggests a rise in the use of the Wolverine peptide in recent years.

A September 2026 preprint analyzed more than 15 million medical records and identified 1,039 people with documented BPC-157 use.

The researchers found a roughly 33-fold increase in new users between 2020 and 2026.

The results, which were based on clinical records, have not yet been published in a peer-reviewed scientific journal.

“The study found that BPC-157 use is largely hidden in clinical narrative rather than conventional prescription data,” said lead study author Venky Soundararajan, PhD, co-founder and Chief Scientific Officer of nference.

“The proportion of newly documented BPC-157 users who were male rose from 57% in 2020 to 70% in 2026, a statistically significant trend,” he told Healthline.

“The user population for BPC-157 peptide is increasingly male in proportion and increasingly younger with each passing year.”

BPC-157 is a synthetic 15-amino-acid peptide marketed and sold online for wellness.

Health claims about it and other peptides have spread on social media and in fitness circles and wellness communities, with high-profile advocates like Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. touting their benefits.

Proponents of BPC-157 claim the injectable peptide promotes injury recovery, pain relief, and tissue healing. Some advocates claim that “stacking” the Wolverine peptide, particularly with TB-500, may enhance its effects.

The “Wolverine” peptide got its nickname from claims that it facilitates rapid healing, echoing the regenerative abilities of the fictional X-Men character of the same name.

This promise of pain relief and recovery may be what gives the Wolverine peptide its appeal.

Soundararajan’s study found that nearly 33% of patients reported using BPC-157 for pain relief. By comparison, just 4% of patients reported using BPC-157 for performance enhancement.

Across the cohort of 1,039 patients, 27% reported improvements.

“Pain relief was the dominant reported benefit, documented in 183 patients,” Soundarajan said. “Wound or tissue healing was reported in 38 patients and functional return in 34 patients.”

“Part of BPC-157’s appeal appears to be to patients who remain symptomatic after chronic, complex or previously treated musculoskeletal problems, signaling a clear clinical unmet need,” he said.

Bert Mandelbaum, MD, a sports medicine specialist, orthopedic surgeon, and co-director of the Regenerative Orthobiologic Center at Cedars-Sinai Orthopaedics and Sports Medicine in Los Angeles, said that everyone experiences pain, but warned about the risks of using the Wolverine peptide to treat it.

“There is zero indication that BPC-157 has any efficacy for pain,” he told Healthline. “There’s zero evidence that shows that it’s good for clinical healing, and we don’t really understand the safety profile of it.”

Human evidence of the possible benefits and safety of the Wolverine peptide is limited.

A 2025 systematic review of current BPC-157 literature found that it was dominated by preclinical research and low-grade human evidence. The review authors said there was insufficient clinical safety data to determine the peptide’s risks.

A small 2021 retrospective study on the effects of injectable BPC-157 on knee pain suggests the peptide could help repair tissue and regrow cartilage. However, the study followed just 16 participants, lacked a control group, and did not assess impacts on function or quality of life.

An even smaller 2025 intravenous safety pilot on BPC-157 use involved 2 participants. While no adverse effects were reported during the short study, the researchers acknowledged that larger studies are needed to confirm safety.

The July 23–24 FDA advisory vote added BPC-157 to the Section 503A bulk drug substances list, allowing pharmacies to include it in compounded medications.

The FDA’s 8-6 vote, with one abstention, greenlit BPC-157 in both free-base and acetate forms.

The recommendation did not make BPC-157 an FDA-approved drug, nor is it a component of an approved product. The federal health agency has yet to make final regulatory decisions on peptides.

“RFK Jr. policies are hurting Americans,” Mandelbaum said.

“This decision is ridiculous. It’s another step in the wrong direction. There’s no basis for any of this stuff.”

The safety profile of the Wolverine peptide for use in humans is largely unknown.

As such, BPC-157 is banned by the World Anti-Doping Agency as an unapproved substance for athletes both in and out of competition.

The FDA notes that peptides like BPC-157 (free base) may be sensitive to product formulation, processing, environmental conditions, and impurities, which could degrade the products and reduce their effectiveness.

The health agency adds that it currently has limited safety information for how peptides like BPC-157 are administered.

Soundararajan explained that his study was unable to estimate the incidence or overall safety of BPC-157 because adverse events were captured retrospectively from routine clinical notes and are likely underreported.

“That said, the medical records examined contain both positive and negative patient experiences,” he said.

“Mood, sleep, skin, injection-site, and gastrointestinal symptoms are concrete safety outcomes among the few examined patient records with available evidence that deserve prospective measurement and discussions by healthcare providers with their patients who report using BPC-157.”

Some experts warn of short-term risks like infection at the injection site, but the long-term effects are not known.

“We don’t have publications of infections yet, but there are anecdotes of this,” Mandelbaum said.

“People are injecting their joints with this stuff, and you have no idea what it is. It’s not standardized. It’s not being regulated by the FDA, and I think people should recognize that. And although we haven’t proven it’s a safety hazard, we know it has no efficacy. So why do it? Why waste your money?”

Experts urge anyone considering BPC-157 or another peptide treatment to discuss it with their doctor first.

You might ask for evidence supporting its use, information on its quality, and whether there are alternatives with established safety and effectiveness profiles.

Mandelbaum noted there are many scientifically proven and safe alternatives to consider.

“We have spent the last 20 years developing an arsenal of biologics from PRP to bone marrow aspirate to adipose tissue to understanding glucosamine and hyaluronic acid,” he said. “We’ve done an amazing job at studying these things. We’re getting to the point where we are understanding the complexities of biologics. It’s exciting, and it’s impactful.”

Mandelbaum vehemently opposed the use of unapproved peptides like BPC-157.

He likened its rise in popularity to a “Ponzi scheme” promoted by “gym rats” and “internet influencers” who “leverage and exploit the gap between hope and knowledge.” Just because the FDA eased restrictions, he said, doesn’t make them any safer or more legitimate.

That said, any use of unapproved peptides should also be disclosed to a doctor.

“A short, open and empathetic clinical conversation can turn invisible consumer use of products like BPC-157 into useful clinical information that can critically help inform current and future care for such patients that ensure the best possible outcomes,” Soundararajan said.

BPC-157 should not be combined with other experimental peptides until research determines the potential for adverse reactions or events. Long-term use of BPC-157 and other peptides marketed for wellness is not advised.

#49. Cutting Back on Ultra-Processed Foods May Help Ease Depression Symptoms

📅 Oct 03, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

Emerging research has linked high consumption of ultra-processed foods (UPFs) with an increased risk of depression. Now, a small study published September 23 in JAMA Psychiatrysuggests that reducing UPF intake may help ease depression symptoms.

There is no universally accepted definition of UPFs. However, a widely used classification system known as NOVA defines them as industrial formulations made primarily from substances extracted or derived from foods, often with additives not typically used in home cooking. Examples include soft drinks, packaged snacks, candy, sausages, hot dogs, and pastries.

Previous research has linked UPFs to an increased risk of depression, anxiety, eating disorders, and other mental health conditions. In the new study, participants reduced their UPF intake by more than 85% and had significantly lower depression scores across three standard measures while following the low-UPF diet.

The participants also had to agree not to change medications, therapy, exercise, or sleep during the study.

Of the 20 participants enrolled in the study, 19 completed it. The average age was 44. Participants followed both their usual diet and a low-UPF diet during separate phases of the study, with the order randomly assigned.

Participants generally found the low-UPF diet acceptable and manageable. They also had lower depression and anxiety scores during the low-UPF diet phase compared with their usual diet.

On one commonly used depression measure, the PHQ-9, average scores declined from 8.68 during the usual diet phase to 5.53 during the low-UPF phase.

The study had several limitations, including its small sample size, reliance on self-reported dietary data, and uncertainty about which aspects of reducing UPFs may have contributed to the changes in symptoms.

The researchers also said that larger studies are needed to determine whether reducing UPF intake can improve depression symptoms.

Michelle Routhenstein, Preventive Cardiology Dietitian at Entirely Nourished, who wasn’t involved in the study, agreed, noting that more research is needed to understand whether reducing UPFs directly improves depression symptoms or whether other aspects of the overall diet may be contributing to the observed benefit.

Dara Houp, PsyD, a licensed clinical psychologist with Pathways Psychological Services in NYC, who wasn’t involved in the study, told Healthline that it’s important to distinguish ultra-processed foods from processed foods more broadly.

Routhenstein agreed that the distinction is important. “Processing is not inherently negative. Many nutritious foods are processed in some way.”

“I would not encourage people to simply label a food as ‘processed’ or ‘unprocessed’ and make decisions based on that label alone. I prefer to look at the overall dietary pattern, the nutritional value of the food, and what role that food plays in the diet. A packaged food can still provide meaningful nutrients and be part of an overall nutritious eating pattern,” she said.

There are various proposed mechanisms for how UPFs may affect mood.

For example, UPFs are often low in fiber, vitamins, and minerals, which can play important roles in brain health.

Houp noted that some ingredients found in UPFs, including artificial sweeteners such as aspartame, may affect the gut microbiome.

“Our gut is a primary location for producing neurotransmitters, responsible for manufacturing the vast majority – upward of 90% – of our body’s serotonin. When the gut microbiome is disrupted, it directly interferes with healthy neurotransmitter production,” she said.

Houp added that diets high in UPFs may be lower in antioxidant-rich foods, such as fruits and vegetables, which could contribute to oxidative stress. Research suggests that people with depression may have lower antioxidant levels and higher markers of oxidative damage.

Routhenstein also noted that researchers are investigating how UPF consumption may influence inflammation, metabolic health, and the gut-brain axis, all of which may have implications for mood.

Reducing your UPF intake can start with small, sustainable changes.

Routhenstein suggested starting by looking at the foods you eat most often rather than trying to overhaul your kitchen.

“Identify one or two foods that are frequent sources of UPFs and consider what you could realistically replace them with. For example, you might swap a daily sugary drink for water or sparkling water, or choose fruit, nuts, yogurt, or another minimally processed option in place of a packaged snack,” she said.

Routhenstein added that you don’t have to eliminate convenience from your diet. Frozen vegetables, canned beans, canned fish, oats, plain yogurt, tofu, and other minimally processed foods can make healthy eating much more practical.

“The goal is not to eat a perfectly unprocessed diet. It is to gradually shift the overall pattern toward more nutrient-dense foods that provide fiber, protein, healthy fats, vitamins, and minerals,” she said.

#52. Letter From the Editor-in-Chief: Is Worrying About Sleep Keeping Us Awake?

📅 Oct 02, 2026  |  Category: Technology  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

As a busy, sleep-deprived, middle-aged father of two young kids, including an irrepressible 8 year old ball of energy who is, without fail, up and ready to take on the world at 6 o’clock every morning (usually with a steady stream of questions about dinosaurs, the natural world and the origins of the universe in his back pocket), do I really need another set of headlines informing me that not only am I falling short in getting the requisite 40 winks a night but I’m also at risk of 83 diseases into the bargain?

Another day, another headline-grabbing sleep study lands, this time linking a greater amount of sleep – specifically sleep of the rapid eye movement (REM) variety, also known as dream sleep – to a lower risk of dozens of diseases.

The recent study published in PLOS Medicine found that REM sleep is linked to a lower risk of 83 diseases, including dementia, Parkinson’s, and multiple sclerosis (MS). As an added bonus, the study also found that a higher amount of deep sleep was associated with a lower risk of seven health conditions, including depression and type 2 diabetes.

So far, so alarming for a self-confessed “bad sleeper”.

Yet also fairly unsurprising. The accepted science, combined with anecdotal experience, has long told us that the right amount of good quality sleep corresponds directly with improved short and long-term health outcomes, both mental and physical. Evidence suggests that 7-9 hours of sleep per night is typically the sweet spot for a healthier life. Where this particular study differs from those that have come before is that scientists used wrist trackers to gather much more detailed information over a multi-year timeline about participants’ sleep stages, including irregular sleep patterns and variations in sleep duration. The findings were drawn from real-world data from more than 95,000 participants in the UK Biobank database.

Now, as the aforementioned and self-proclaimed VBP (very busy person – if you know, you know), who works long hours across UK and US timezones for the day job whilst serving as a sub-par encyclopedia for the real boss (the aforementioned 8 year old) outside of work, the general demands and pressures of daily life are more than sufficient to make consistently good sleep something that is all too often out of reach.

I know this to be true because the wearable I use reminds me of the fact with cold, hard data on a daily, if not hourly, basis. One of the first things my wife and I do each morning (after fending off existential questions from a third grader) is hawkishly plug into our Oura app to trade sleep scores and discuss what did or didn’t contribute to our invariably bad or, very rarely, good night’s sleep. Deep sleep, REM sleep, resting heart rate, heart rate variability, latency, even sleep debt – a laundry list of data points that we pore over in a futile attempt to make our sleep consistency optimal whilst living a life that doesn’t readily allow for it. Scientists have even given this phenomenon a name – Orthosomnia – where the anxiety of analyzing and perfecting sleep scores actually disrupts rest and intensifies poor sleep, and in the most extreme cases, insomnia.

It turns out we’re not alone. Results of a Gallup poll from 2024 – with the rather on-the-nose headline “Americans sleeping less, more stressed” – showed Americans report averaging 6.5 hours of sleep per night, comfortably below the recommended baseline of 7+ hours. Perhaps more tellingly, in a survey from 2024, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine found that nearly 70% of Americans report losing sleep due to anxiety.

Which brings me back to my original point. Anxiety-induced stress makes for the perfect bedfellow (pun intended) for a bad night’s sleep. The kind of anxiety-induced stress that can be both caused and amplified by obsessing over wearables data and a steady torrent of sleep-related content that aims to scaremonger rather than inform.

In fairness to the UK Biobank study, it is unequivocally important and unique research steeped in actionable real-world data and peer-reviewed medical science. And yet we live in a world increasingly awash with AI-generated content, sporting sensationalist headlines and powered by algorithms designed with an inherent imbalance to engage rather than empower. In that environment, gold-standard scientific work like this is all too often reduced to the lowest common denominator.

If you’ll indulge a minor tooting of our own horn here, this is precisely the inverse of how the team of wonderfully talented editorial misfits at Healthline Media thinks about and creates health information. We take research-backed science and our embedded clinical rigor and apply it to every aspect of our work. But we never lose sight of the fact that behind every study, there’s someone lying awake in the early hours running the math on their own risk factors, and that what they need in that moment is recognition that we are all at different stages on that journey, and a helping hand along the path to better health.

Life isn’t linear, sleep isn’t linear, and health is certainly not linear. Some nights I’ll get my seven hours. Plenty of others I won’t. And that is ok. Because either way, my alarm clock has a favorite dinosaur and a nonnegotiable 6 a.m. start time, and no peer-reviewed study or scary headline is going to change that. So I’ll take the data and the empathy, leave the anxiety, and see you all again at dawn.

If there’s something keeping you awake at night, or you just want to learn more about the wonderful world of sleep, we have a wealth of science-backed information at our dedicated Sleep Health hub. There you can also find Healthline’s Better Sleep Challenge – a three-week series designed to help you sleep better, feel more rested, and wake up ready to thrive each day.

He’ll soon be launching a dedicated Letter from the Editor newsletter coming to an inbox near you later in the fall.

#54. Low-Energy Diet Plus Structured Exercise Reverses Early Onset Diabetes

📅 Oct 01, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

A new study has found that a combination of a low-energy diet and structured exercise reversed early onset type 2 diabetes in about half of participants.

The research focuses on adults under 45 years old who developed type 2 diabetes early in life. The authors presented their findings at the European Association for the Study of Diabetes (EASD) Annual Meeting, held September 28 to October 2. The study was published on October 1 in The Lancet Regional Health Americas.

Type 2 diabetes is a condition in which the body becomes less able to respond to insulin or has difficulty producing it, leading to elevated blood sugar and low energy. If untreated or not properly managed, it can lead to serious complications such as vision problems, kidney disease, and nerve damage.

These findings offer hope for better treatment options for people with this condition, which the authors say is known for causing faster health decline when compared to later-onset diabetes.

The study was conducted at three centers in England and Canada, linked with the University of Leicester, McGill University, and the University of Alberta.

Researchers selected adults ages 18 to 45 who had early onset type 2 diabetes and obesity but were not using insulin therapy.

A total of 96 participants were randomly divided into two groups: one received usual care, and the other took part in a program combining a low-energy diet with supervised and independent exercise.

The intervention group followed a strict diet of 800-900 calories per day, consisting of 30% protein, 50% carbohydrates, and 20% fat. Participants were encouraged to drink at least 2 liters of calorie-free fluids daily and were given a fiber-based laxative if needed.

For the first two weeks, the diet was entirely based on meal replacements (Optifast). From week 3 to week 12, the same calorie limit was maintained, with greater food variety, including one meal per day made from regular foods such as protein sources, one portion of fruit, and two portions of non-starchy vegetables.

The exercise program involved supervised aerobic and resistance training twice a week and one session of independent exercise per week during the first 12 weeks. After this, from weeks 13 to 24, participants followed a weight-maintenance diet and exercised independently.

Participants stopped all glucose– and blood pressure-lowering medications at the start of the diet unless they had a specific kidney condition called albuminuria (high levels of a protein called albumin in the urine).

The main goal of the study was to determine how many participants achieved remission of their diabetes after 24 weeks. Remission was defined as having a blood sugar level below the diabetes threshold (measured by glycated hemoglobin or HbA1c less than 6.5%) and not taking any diabetes medication for at least 12 weeks.

The study also examined secondary outcomes such as fitness levels, body composition, and heart health.

After 24 weeks, 54% of participants in the diet and exercise group achieved remission, compared with only 4% in the usual care group.

They also lost weight. The diet and exercise group lost an average of 8.4 kilograms (about 18.5 pounds), while the usual care group lost only 1.2 kilograms (about 2.6 pounds).

After adjusting for various factors, the diet-and-exercise group lost about 7.5 kilograms more than the control group.

Additionally, most of this weight loss was fat, with about 6.7 kilograms of fat lost at 24 weeks, which the researchers note is important for improving diabetes.

According to the researchers, the intervention helped preserve muscle mass, which is usually lost during weight loss. This is significant because maintaining muscle is important for long-term health, strength, and metabolism.

They further highlighted that early onset type 2 diabetes can cause serious health problems at a young age.

However, their study shows that a combined approach of diet and exercise can not only help people lose weight and improve health but can also lead to real remission of diabetes, without needing medication.

This is important because early onset type 2 diabetes usually worsens quickly and is harder to manage than diabetes that starts later in life.

The researchers additionally believe their approach could be especially helpful for younger adults who may be planning to have a child, as managing diabetes early is critical for health during and after pregnancy.

The study’s success in a multi-ethnic population also adds to its significance, showing the program could work for diverse groups.

The researchers noted that most people in the diet-and-exercise group experienced side effects early in the program, including constipation, headaches, dizziness, and fatigue. However, these were mostly mild and resolved over time.

Avery Zenker, a registered dietitian and medical writer, explained the importance of this work. “We have evidence that type 2 diabetes doesn’t have to be an inevitably progressive disease,” Zenker told Healthline.

“This can give hope for people with diabetes and help empower them to make impactful lifestyle changes.”

Zenker, who was not involved in the study, added that type 2 diabetes remission is not only possible but more likely in younger people who tend to have a shorter disease duration and a less complicated health history.

Jennifer Pallian, BSc, a registered dietitian and founder of Foodess, who wasn’t involved in the research, said that you can put these findings into action right now.

First, you can start a lower-calorie diet. “A reasonable target used in the research was to reduce daily calorie intake by about 500–750 calories, with the goal of losing at least 5% of starting body weight,” she told Healthline.

Zenker noted there are other factors to consider besides the number of calories. “There’s a big difference between a low calorie diet that’s predominantly whole foods compared to one that’s mostly ultra-processed and nutrient-poor,” she said.

“It’s important to consider other recommendations like whole foods, high fiber, and reduced intake of ultra-processed foods.”

Along with making dietary changes, Pallian advised working toward getting at least 150 minutes of moderate-intensity activity per week. “It also helps to make the plan measurable,” she said. “Set specific nutrition and activity goals, keep track of what and how much you eat, and record your physical activity too.”

Pallian suggested taking note of situations where it’s harder to stay on track so you can spot patterns and make adjustments.

Zenker further noted the importance of starting small and not feeling as if going to the gym is the only way to get exercise.

“Some simple ways one might start to increase daily activity could include adding a short body weight routine in the morning, taking a short walk after a meal, or dancing to music at home,” she said. “Even activities like yard work or cleaning the house can count as physical activity.”

Finally, Pallian advised regular visits with a healthcare professional to monitor blood sugar and blood pressure. These values may improve with weight loss, which may require your doctor to adjust your medication regimen.

#56. Wall Squats, Planks Once a Week May Help Lower Your Blood Pressure

📅 Oct 01, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

Isometric exercises, even if done only occasionally, could help improve blood pressure, according to a new study.

Researchers report that study participants who did these types of exercises once or twice a week saw reductions in blood pressure over a short period.

They added that participants who engaged in isometric exercises three times a week maintained improvements in blood pressure over a longer period.

The researchers said that previous studies had indicated that isometric exercise training reduces blood pressure more than other forms of exercise and even some drug treatments.

However, they said previous research had not established how often isometric exercise training needs to be performed to maintain these health benefits.

Isometric exercises involve a person holding a pose for a few minutes at a time. Examples include wall squats and floor planks.

The researchers said their study provides important information for people who don’t have time to exercise daily.

The researchers wrote that the study “offers evidence that lower frequency [isometric exercise training] can help maintain short-term [blood pressure] reductions, providing a pragmatic option for individuals with limited time or adherence challenges.”

They published their findings on September 29 in BMJ Open Sport and Exercise Medicine.

Experts not involved in the research said it provides helpful guidance for people who want to improve their blood pressure.

“The main takeaway is that you don’t need to exercise every day — or even several times a week — to experience significant blood pressure benefits from isometric exercise,” said Michael Fredericson, MD, a professor of orthopaedic surgery at Stanford Health Care.

“We already knew that performing isometric training for even just a few weeks can lower blood pressure,” added Amanda Paluch, PhD, an associate professor of kinesiology at the University of Massachusetts Amherst and chair of the American Heart Association’s Physical Activity Committee. “This study adds to the research by looking at how much training is needed to maintain those gains.”

“It is encouraging to see measurable improvements in blood pressure with a relatively brief and infrequent exercise routine,” said Jennifer Wong, MD, a cardiologist and medical director of Non-Invasive Cardiology at MemorialCare Heart and Vascular Institute at Orange Coast Medical Center in California. “These findings suggest that even small, consistent efforts can make a meaningful difference.”

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) estimates that nearly half of adults in the United States have high blood pressure. That’s about 120 million people.

The condition, also known as hypertension, increases the risk of heart disease and stroke.

High blood pressure occurs when the force of blood pushing through blood vessels is consistently too high.

Blood pressure readings consist of two numbers. One is the systolic pressure (top number), which measures pressure when your heart beats. The second is diastolic pressure (the bottom number), which measures the pressure between beats.

Treatment usually involves medications such as beta-blockers and ACE inhibitors, as well as lifestyle factors such as a healthy diet and exercise.

In their study, the researchers aimed to determine how much isometric exercise was needed to lower and maintain blood pressure.

The researchers recruited 100 adults with an average age of 37. Subjects included male and female participants with normal-to-high-normal systolic blood pressure (120–140 mm Hg) who engaged in less than 150 minutes per week of moderate physical activity.

The participants also had no injuries or illnesses, were not taking and had not previously taken blood pressure medication, were not smokers, and did not consume more than 14 units of alcohol per week.

The participants were divided into five groups. One group served as a control and did no isometric exercise, while the other four groups followed the same four-week home-based lower-body isometric training program.

The regimen consisted of four sessions of isometric wall squat exercise, separated by two minutes of seated rest, performed three times a week.

During the next four weeks, the participants were split into four training schedules.

One group continued to exercise three times a week. A second group exercised twice a week, while a third group exercised once a week. The remaining group stopped the exercises entirely.

Blood pressure, heart rate, and other cardiac measurements were taken at the start of the study and after four weeks and eight weeks.

The researchers reported that the four groups who completed the initial four-week exercise routine showed significant reductions in systolic and diastolic blood pressure compared to the control group.

Over the next four weeks, the researchers found no statistically significant difference in diastolic blood pressure among the groups.

However, the researchers reported that participants who continued isometric exercise training three times a week achieved the greatest reduction in systolic blood pressure compared with baseline. That group also maintained improvements in other cardiac measures.

The group that had stopped doing isometric exercises after the initial four-week period showed a reversal of their blood pressure improvements.

The researchers wrote that the study “demonstrates that while lower frequency [isometric exercise training] may be sufficient to maintain [blood pressure] reductions over the short term, higher frequencies are required to preserve these benefits and the associated vascular and cardiac autonomic adaptations.”

The researchers noted that the eight-week study period was limited and that further research is needed. They also acknowledged that the participants were from a relatively small cross-section of people.

There are several ways isometric exercises may help lower blood pressure.

“Repeat isometric exercises (contracting a muscle and holding a position) seem to change how the blood vessels regulate pressure between workouts,” said Kevin Shah, MD, a cardiologist and program director of Heart Failure Outreach at MemorialCare Heart & Vascular Institute at Long Beach Medical Center in California.

“Blood pressure comes down to how hard the heart pumps and how much resistance the blood meets in the vessels,” added Paluch.

“Research suggests isometric training works mainly on that second part. It helps your blood vessels relax and open up, so blood flows through more easily.”

“Isometric exercise seems to promote cardiovascular adaptations that can lower blood pressure,” explained Fredericson. “Over time, this training can enhance the cardiovascular system’s ability to regulate blood pressure.”

Experts interviewed by Healthline say that the wall squats used in the study are one of the most beneficial isometric exercises.

They also recommend static leg extensions, handgrip exercises, planks, and exercises that involve sustained muscle contraction.

“Start slowly, with manageable goals,” Wong said. “Even one or two short sessions per week can be a starting point,” she said. “Begin with brief holds, allow adequate rest between sets and sessions, and gradually build up as strength and comfort improve. The goal is to establish a routine one can maintain consistently.”

Paluch recommended starting small and keeping it simple. She says 10 to 20 minutes per session is sufficient.

She suggested four 2-minute holds of a wall sit or handgrip squeeze with 2 minutes of rest between each hold. Those can be done two to three days a week.

“The message is that consistency matters,” she said. “If you’re able to maintain three times a week, that appears to be the best way to maintain the gain, but it’s not an all-or-nothing situation.”

“You don’t have to maintain the initial pace forever to get something out of the training, especially when life gets in the way and adherence or time becomes a challenge,” Paluch added. “Even getting in one day a week is better than nothing.”

For those just getting started with exercise, Shah recommended starting with a short walk before adding isometric exercise.

“A 10-minute walk on most days is a manageable first step,” Shah said. “Gradually increase the length of walks and then add strength training. Consistency matters more than an exhaustive routine that is not sustainable. Remember: Progress and not perfection.”

Fredericson advised starting slowly and building from there.

“For someone completely inactive, even 5 to 10 minutes of movement per day is a meaningful starting point,” he said. “Walking is an excellent option because it requires little to no equipment or preparation. From there, they can incorporate strength training two or three days a week.”

#57. Insomnia Isn’t Just Exhausting — It May Raise Your Risk of Stroke, Dementia

📅 Sep 30, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

Insomnia may do more than leave you tired. A review published in Sleep Medicine Reviews on September 7 has linked it to a higher risk of stroke, depression, and hospitalization.

Insomnia is characterized by difficulty falling or staying asleep despite having an adequate opportunity to sleep and typically involves impaired daytime functioning.

“Sleep is such an important health factor when we think about overall well-being,” Joseph You, MD, cardiologist with Manhattan Cardiology in NYC, who wasn’t involved in the study, told Healthline.

He noted that sleep deprivation and poor-quality sleep have long been linked to adverse cardiovascular events and other health problems.

Insomnia is the most common sleep disorder in adults. Chronic insomnia affects approximately 22% of the general population.

“Chronic insomnia is generally characterized by ongoing sleep difficulty despite having an adequate opportunity to sleep, rather than simply having an occasional bad night,” noted Christopher Yi, MD, board certified vascular surgeon at MemorialCare Orange Coast Medical Center in Fountain Valley, CA, who wasn’t involved in the study.

To examine the association between insomnia and stroke, the researchers identified nine studies involving more than 1.3 million people. Six were included in the meta-analysis, which found that insomnia was associated with a 26% higher risk of stroke.

It is important to keep in mind that association does not equal causation. However, several factors may help explain the link between insomnia and stroke.

Persistent sleep disruptions can trigger overactivation of the sympathetic nervous system.

The sympathetic nervous system is part of the body’s involuntary nervous system that triggers your “fight or flight” response in stressful or dangerous situations.

“Being too wired can impair sleep, and impaired sleep, in turn, can make us too wired. A state of higher adrenaline and cortisol raises blood pressure, blood sugar, and increases inflammation, all of which have serious health effects,” said Alex Dimitriu, MD, double board certified in Psychiatry and Sleep Medicine, and founder of Menlo Park Psychiatry & Sleep Medicine, who wasn’t involved in the study.

The review authors noted that these changes can not only raise blood pressure and heart rate but can also increase vascular tone while reducing heart rate variability. Continuous cortisol release can also promote hypertension, systemic inflammation, and disrupt glucose and lipid metabolism.

Together, these changes may promote plaque formation and compromise vascular health, potentially contributing to a higher risk of stroke.

“I would emphasize that this study demonstrates an association and does not prove that insomnia directly causes stroke or other cardiovascular events,” cautioned Yi.

“There was significant variability among the studies, and factors such as underlying medical conditions and the use of sleep medications could influence the results. The authors themselves caution that an independent causal role for insomnia has not been established.”

The researchers also looked at five studies examining the association between insomnia and hospitalization. The studies included mostly older adults and ranged in size from 1,101 to 484,707 participants.

Three of the studies, involving 19,745 participants, were included in a meta-analysis. Researchers found that people with insomnia had a 28% higher likelihood of hospitalization for any reason.

The researchers noted that insomnia may be a key marker of poor health and may exacerbate the severity of underlying health conditions.

Dimitriu said a good indication that it may be time to seek help is when sleep problems begin to affect your life, health, or happiness. If you are unable to sleep at least 6–7 hours when given the opportunity, or regularly feel tired or sleepy during the day, he recommends speaking with a healthcare professional.

You recommended seeking help as soon as you begin to suspect you may have insomnia. Doing so may also help identify other risk factors or lifestyle habits that could be affecting your sleep and overall health.

The researchers reviewed six meta-analyses examining the association between insomnia and depression and other mental health disorders.

Although most were rated as having low or critically low methodological quality, the findings consistently linked insomnia with a higher risk of depression.

According to the researchers, chronic insomnia can lead to daytime fatigue, attention issues, and reduced executive functioning. These factors can increase rumination, negative thinking, and maladaptive coping strategies, which are all risk factors for depression and anxiety.

Separately, two meta-analyses found that insomnia was associated with a higher risk of suicidal ideation, suicide attempts, and suicide death.

Sleep disturbances are among several risk factors for suicide, including independently of depression.

This isn’t to say that insomnia causes depression or suicidal behaviors, only that it may be one among various risk factors.

The researchers reviewed five meta-analyses examining the association between insomnia and dementia. The analyses included between 5 and 39 studies and ranged from 36,005 to more than 3.1 million participants, with follow-up periods ranging from 2 to 40 years.

Overall, most of the analyses linked insomnia with a higher risk of dementia. Three also found that insomnia was associated with a higher risk of Alzheimer’s disease.

The researchers noted several possible explanations for the association. Sleep disturbances may contribute to inflammation and impaired clearance of substances such as amyloid-beta and tau from the brain, processes that could potentially contribute to neurodegeneration.

However, these findings show an association between insomnia and dementia and do not establish that insomnia causes dementia.

#58. 8-Hour Time-Restricted Eating Window May Lead to Greater Weight Loss

📅 Sep 30, 2026  |  Category: Health  |  Read Source →

Follow Healthline on our socials for all things health.

Limiting your eating to an 8-hour window may result in more weight loss than a 12-hour window, according to a new study published in the journal Nutrition & Diabetes on September 12.

Time-restricted eating (TRE) is a form of intermittent fasting that limits eating to a set daily window, such as 8, 10, or 12 hours, without necessarily changing what you eat.

The eating window is followed by fasting for the remaining hours. Common approaches include 16:8, which involves 16 hours of fasting and 8 hours of eating, and early TRE, which involves eating earlier in the day.

The researchers used data from 124 adults across five TRE trials lasting between 3 and 6 months. At baseline, participants had an average eating window of about 15 hours and at least one component of metabolic syndrome.

Their food and beverage intake was tracked using an app.

Participants were assigned either to a TRE group or to the control group. The control group participants maintained their habitual intake, received nutritional counseling, or were given guidance on healthy eating.

Those in the TRE group limited their eating windows to 8, 10, or 12 hours, with no restrictions on dietary quality or energy intake.

After 3–6 months, the researchers found that participants assigned to an 8-hour eating window lost about 3.7 pounds (1.7 kg) more than those assigned to a 12-hour window and about 5 pounds (2.3 kg) more than those in the control group.

However, there was no statistically significant difference in weight loss between the 8-hour and the 10-hour windows. The 8-hour window also showed no significant impact on blood pressure, glucose, or cholesterol.

“The findings are interesting and add to the evidence that shortening the daily eating window may help with weight loss, but I would be cautious about concluding that eight hours is the optimal window,” Dalia Perelman, MS, RDN, CDCES, research dietitian at Stanford Medicine, who wasn’t involved in the study, told Healthline.

“One finding I find particularly interesting is that people who shortened their eating window more relative to their usual pattern tended to lose more weight. This suggests that the benefit may not be about everyone following exactly the same eight-hour window, but rather about meaningfully shortening one’s habitual eating period,” she added.

The researchers also found that the 10-hour eating window significantly reduced systolic blood pressure compared with the 12-hour window. There were no significant differences between the eating windows for glucose or lipids.

However, a 2020 study found that a 10-hour eating window reduced weight, blood pressure, and cholesterol, among adults with metabolic syndrome who were already taking medication.

“[These] effects are generally modest, and TRE should be viewed as one potential component of an overall lifestyle approach rather than a treatment on its own,” said Perelman.

“I think TRE is best viewed as one possible tool rather than a magic formula. When we eat appears to matter, but what and how much we eat still matter as well,” she added.

“A nutritious diet, limiting highly processed foods, avoiding eating late at night, and finding an eating pattern that is enjoyable and sustainable are probably more important than rigidly adhering to a specific number of hours.”

Perelman also told Healthline that “Older adults at risk for muscle loss should also make sure they are consuming adequate calories and protein.”

She added that people with diabetes who use insulin or medications that can cause hypoglycemia should discuss TRE with their healthcare professional because changing when they eat may require medication adjustments.

As with any eating plan, time-restricted eating should be realistic and sustainable for your lifestyle.

Perelman noted that health is about more than what and when you eat. She said that sharing meals with family and friends, social connection, and enjoyment of food are important too.

A rigid eating schedule that interferes with those things may not be beneficial overall.

“Rather than focusing on achieving a precise eight-hour window, I would prioritize a balanced, nutrient-rich diet low in highly processed foods and, when possible, finish eating earlier in the evening,” Perelman said.
